How Chords Come From Scales

Learn how every triad you play is built directly from a major scale — starting with stacking scale degrees into a chord, then generalizing into the full I–ii–iii–IV–V–vi–vii° pattern that governs any key.

From Scale to Chord: Stacking the 1st, 3rd, and 5th

A triad isn't a shape you memorize separately from the scale — it's three notes pulled straight out of it. As one video on chord construction puts it, "these chords come from these notes... the seven notes are the only notes in all seven chords." To build the most basic chord in a key, you don't add anything: you take the scale, number its notes 1 through 7, and pick out the 1st, 3rd, and 5th.

SourceThe Most Important Piece of Music Theory - Chords of a Key

1

Number the C major scale

Label every note of C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C by its scale degree first.

C major scale: C is 1, D is 2, E is 3, F is 4, G is 5, A is 6, B is 7, and back to C. Write the letters out and put the number under each one before you touch a chord.

2

Pick out degrees 1, 3, and 5

Skip every other scale note and you're left with the chord.

Skip degree 2, take degree 3 (E). Skip degree 4, take degree 5 (G). You're left with C, E, and G — root, third, fifth, stacked in thirds as they climb in pitch. One guitar-scales lesson names this exact recipe the major triad: "using one three and five." Play those three notes together and you have a C major chord, built from nothing but the C major scale itself.

SourceGUITAR SCALES Made Easy

C, E, and G — scale degrees 1, 3, and 5 — stacked as a root-position triad on the D–A–E strings.

Note

The seven notes of a major scale are the only notes used across all seven chords built from it — one source calls them "the DNA of the sound." Building the C chord from C-E-G doesn't borrow a single note from outside the C major scale.

Major or Minor? The Third Tells the Story

You already know a major triad is Root–3–5. What actually makes it major is the size of that first gap: root to third has to be a major third — four half steps. Take C, E, G: C to E is a major third, so the chord reads major, and as long as the fifth (G) is a clean perfect fifth, the whole triad is confidently major. Change nothing else about the shape — just shrink that one interval by a half step — and the entire chord's identity flips. That's the whole story of major vs. minor: it lives in the third, not in the root or the fifth.

SourceI Didn't Know Where CHORDS Came From Until I Knew THIS

The root-to-third gap in a major triad is always a major third (4 half steps), whatever key the picker is set to — try other keys and the shape just slides.

SourceGUITAR SCALES Made Easy

Note

In any major triad, the gap from the third up to the fifth is always a minor third — even though the gap from the root to the third is major. The two outer intervals don't match, and that asymmetry is normal, not a mistake.

SourceI Didn't Know Where CHORDS Came From Until I Knew THIS

To get a minor triad, you don't need a new shape — you take any major triad and flatten the middle note, the third, by one fret. Root and fifth stay exactly where they are. So a C major triad's E becomes E♭, and the chord's whole character shifts from bright to dark without moving your root or fifth fingers at all.

SourceGUITAR SCALES Made Easy

Same shape as the major triad with only the middle note nudged down one fret — root and fifth haven't moved.

SourceGUITAR SCALES Made Easy

Push the idea one step further and remove the third completely, leaving only root and fifth. That's a power chord — R and 5, nothing else. Because there's no third at all, a power chord is neither major nor minor; it doesn't answer the question the third exists to answer, it just sidesteps it.

SourceGUITAR SCALES Made Easy

Root on the A string, fifth two strings up — just those two notes. No third means no major or minor quality at all.

SourceGUITAR SCALES Made Easy

See the major triad turned minor, then stripped to a power chord

There's a fourth case where the third's story gets stranger. Build a triad on the seventh degree of a major scale and you get something like B, D, F — B to D is a minor third, but D to F is also a minor third. Stacking two minor thirds instead of one is what makes this chord diminished rather than plain minor, and it's why it gets its own lowercase-with-a-circle marking instead of just a lowercase Roman numeral.

SourceI Didn't Know Where CHORDS Came From Until I Knew THIS

Note

A regular minor triad has one minor third (root to third) and one major third (third to fifth). A diminished triad like B–D–F has two minor thirds stacked back to back — that's the one structural difference that earns it the separate ° symbol.

B–D–F, built on the major scale's 7th degree: minor third stacked on minor third is what makes this one diminished rather than minor.

Every Scale Degree Gets a Chord: I–ii–iii–IV–V–vi–vii°

Once you have a major scale laid out — say C D E F G A B — you can build a triad on top of every single note by stacking two more notes a third above it, skipping every other letter. Do that seven times, once per scale degree, and you get seven chords. As one video puts it, you start on a root, "jump over" to collect the third, then jump over again to collect the fifth: C→E→G gives you C major, D→F→A gives you D minor, E→G→B gives you E minor, and so on all the way up the scale.

SourceI Didn't Know Where CHORDS Came From Until I Knew THIS

Watch the third-stacking demonstration

What matters is that the quality of each triad — major, minor, or diminished — is not random. It falls out automatically from where the half steps sit inside the major scale. As one source explains, in any major key the interval from the third note to the fourth note is always a half step, and so is the interval from the seventh note back to the root — everywhere else the gap is a whole step. That fixed pattern of whole and half steps is exactly what forces chord ii to come out minor, chord V to come out major, and chord vii to come out diminished, no matter which key you're in.

Note

Chords I, IV, and V always come out major and are called the primary triads — they're the source of the most important chord movement, cadences. Chords ii, iii, and vi always come out minor, and chord vii always comes out diminished. This holds in every major key, not just C.

Primary vs Secondary Triads

Not every chord in a key pulls the same weight. Stack a triad on scale degree 1 and you get the tonic (I) — home base. Stack one on degree 4 and on degree 5 and you get IV and V, the two chords that push away from home and back to it. As one source puts it, chords built on 1, 4, and 5 are the primary triads because they're "the most important ones" — they're where the strongest chord-to-chord movements, the cadences, come from.

Stack the major scale into triads and the quality pattern is fixed: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished — the same order in any key.

That fixed pattern — major, minor, major, minor, diminished, if you skip straight to naming the primary/secondary split — is why ii, iii, and vi are called the secondary triads: they're minor, they support the primary chords rather than driving the harmony themselves, and swapping keys never changes their function, only their letter names. Change from C to G and I-IV-V becomes G, C, and D, but the shape — major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished — never moves.

SourceI Didn't Know Where CHORDS Came From Until I Knew THIS

Note

Two sources describe the seventh chord differently. "I Didn't Know Where CHORDS Came From Until I Knew THIS" calls it simply diminished — the wobbly chord at the end of the row. "The Most Important Piece of Music Theory" goes further: built as a seventh chord it's a minor seven flat five, also called half-diminished, not a full diminished seventh. Both agree it sits on scale degree 7 and carries the least stable sound in the key — they just describe the chord at different levels of detail (triad vs. seventh chord).

The vii° chord both sources are describing — a diminished triad, stacked from two minor thirds. Shape shown for the reader's chosen key.

Modes: The Same Notes, a Different Starting Point

Every mode is hiding in a major scale you already know — you don't need a new set of notes, just a different note to call "home." Take the C major scale: C D E F G A B. Play the same seven notes but start and end on D instead of C — D E F G A B C — and you haven't added a single new pitch. What's changed is which note the ear treats as the root, and that shift alone turns the C major scale into D Dorian.

The reason this matters for chords is the same reason the I–IV–V pattern worked in the first place: a scale's sound comes from the gaps between its notes, not the note names themselves. One source puts it directly — modes "come from this as well," meaning the same major-scale scaffolding that generates I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii° also generates every mode, just measured from a different starting point in the row.

SourceThe Most Important Piece of Music Theory - Chords of a Key

That row above is the intervallic structure of the major scale itself — the fixed pattern of whole and half steps, always described this way regardless of key. As one source notes, the key of G major has an F♯ in it, but its intervallic structure is still 1-2-3-4-5-6-7. The notes change from key to key; the structure that defines "major" does not.

SourceGuitar scales explained like you're 5

4

Pick the parent scale and a new starting degree

Nothing about the note collection changes — only where you begin.

Play the notes of C major (C D E F G A B) but start on D and end an octave higher on D: D E F G A B C.

SourceGuitar scales explained like you're 5

5

Renumber the intervals against the new root

Compare each note's distance from the new root, not the old one.

Measured from D, F is only 3 half steps up (a minor third, not a major third) and C is only 10 half steps up (a minor seventh, not a major seventh). Every other note lines up with a major-scale interval from D.

SourceGuitar scales explained like you're 5

Note

D Dorian shares every note with C major — no sharps, no flats added. What makes it Dorian is that, measured from D as the root, the third and seventh land a half step flatter than a major scale from D would give you.

SourceGuitar scales explained like you're 5

D Dorian is built from the same seven notes as C major, but measured from D the flagged notes (F and C) land a half step flatter than a D major scale would — the mode's ♭3 and ♭7.
